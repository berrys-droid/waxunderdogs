Bio — long (170 words)

for features, reviews, interviews

Wax Underdogs is the songwriting project of Christian Berry, who spent six years in Ukraine and Crimea, trained as a translator, and now works in technical publications in Melbourne, Florida. The songs are slow and lyric-forward, and they keep returning to the same subject: what a person holds onto when there is nothing left to hold.

The debut single, "Hold," adapts Kipling's "If—" into a survival song with a chorus that isn't in the poem. "Anam" is sung in Crimean Tatar, built on a lullaby chorus carried in his wife Elvira's family's oral tradition. "Kneel" draws on the traditional Ubi caritas and the foot-washing in John 13; "Let Not Your Heart" on John 14.

Berry writes the words and the underlying music and renders the recordings with Suno. He discloses this on every release, in the credits and in the copy, and has been public about getting the attribution wrong on the first pass and correcting it.

How the music is made

Stated plainly, because every outlet asks and it should come from the artist rather than be discovered:

quotable — attribute to Christian Berry

"I write the lyrics and the underlying music — the chord progression, the structure — and I record that before the session. Suno renders it: the sung melody, the lead guitar, the voice, the instrumentation.

I disclose it everywhere because the alternative is that somebody finds out. I also got the credits wrong on the first release and had to correct them publicly. The line between writing something and prompting something is harder to draw than people arguing about it seem to think, and I'd rather be precise about mine than defensive about it."

Three angles worth writing about

01

The disclosure, handled in the open

A songwriter publishing partial-AI records in the same season Spotify began showing AI credits to listeners — who got his own credits wrong, said so, and corrected them. The authorship question argued from inside it rather than about it.

02

A lullaby that survived a deportation

"Anam" carries a Crimean Tatar chorus in a wording particular to one family's variation — a language whose speakers were deported from Crimea in 1944. A song about what a language holds when the people who speak it are moved.

03

Kipling, rewritten as a hymn

"If—" is a poem about self-mastery. "Hold" answers it: the same virtues, but the strength borrowed rather than owned — "there is a Love that will not let you fall."
